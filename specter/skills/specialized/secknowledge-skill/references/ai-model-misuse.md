### Image Information Fabrication

> Risk ID: GAARM.0031.003
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Using technologies such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), attackers can generate realistic fake images. These fake images may be used for false advertising, fabricating evidence, online fraud, and similar purposes. Additionally, image information fabrication may lead to the leakage of personal identity information. By analyzing personal photos, social media information, and other publicly available data, attackers can use AI to generate realistic facial images and use them to impersonate others. This poses serious risks to personal privacy and data security.

**Attack Cases**

Case | Description
--- | ---
Case 1 | A finance employee received an email impersonating the CFO and was invited to a video conference in which all participants were Deepfake false images created from publicly available video and audio clips, causing the company to lose HKD 200 million (approximately RMB 180 million).
Case 2 | AI-generated images of false information raised the credibility of content inconsistent with the facts, leading to serious public opinion consequences.

**Attack Risks**

- Misleading information: fake images may be used to spread false information and influence public opinion.
- Reputational damage: enterprises or individuals may be defamed through fake images, affecting their reputation and potentially causing financial losses.
- Legal consequences: publishing fake images may result in legal liability, especially in cases involving defamation or privacy violations.

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Content review | Use image recognition and content review tools to detect fake or tampered images.
Watermarking technology | Clearly label generated images to inform users that they are not from a real source.
Source verification | Use image forensics tools to examine image metadata and editing history.
Policy development | Establish clear policies and legal frameworks targeting the use and distribution of fake images.

### Multimodal Content Compliance Security Risk

> Risk ID: GAARM.0062
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Multimodal content compliance security risk refers to the security threat that content generated by multimodal models may violate laws, regulations, ethical standards, or platform policies. This risk involves non-compliant content in various forms including text, images, audio, and video; traditional single-modality compliance detection mechanisms are ill-equipped to handle the complex cross-modal violation scenarios. Multimodal content may bypass conventional detection through metaphorical suggestion, cross-modal implication, deep semantic association, and similar means, generating outputs containing false information, hate speech, violent content, adult content, or other non-compliant material, posing a serious threat to social order and user safety.

**Attack Cases**

Case | Description
--- | ---
Case 1 | After the image generation feature of Grok—the AI chatbot developed by Elon Musk's company xAI and integrated into the social platform X—went live, users abused it to create sexually suggestive and unauthorized nude images (including of minors), triggering global regulatory investigations and platform rectification.
Case 2 | On the evening of December 22, 2025, large numbers of users reported that pornographic content appeared in Kuaishou live-streaming rooms, including the distribution of obscene videos, vulgar performances, and other inappropriate live content, with some rooms attracting tens of thousands of viewers. After the news spread, some users filed police reports, and authorities stated they had received multiple public tip-offs. The platform responded that the phenomenon was caused by an attack by black-hat/gray-hat actors and that it had taken emergency action and reported the matter to public security authorities.

Risk Manifestations

- Cross-modal non-compliant content generation: generating multimodal content that violates laws and regulations
- Covert non-compliant information dissemination: spreading non-compliant information through cross-modal implication
- Deep-fake non-compliant content: generating false and harmful multimodal content
- Content compliance detection bypass: exploiting cross-modal characteristics to circumvent existing detection mechanisms
- Multimodal misleading content: generating multimodal content that is misleading or harmful

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Cross-modal compliance detection | Build a multimodal content compliance detection system; implement cross-modal semantic association analysis to detect covert non-compliant content and implied information.
Multi-dimensional content analysis | Simultaneously analyze multiple modalities such as text, images, and audio; establish cross-modal consistency checking mechanisms; implement multi-level compliance assessment.
Real-time content monitoring | Establish a real-time multimodal content monitoring system; implement dynamic compliance detection; build rapid response mechanisms for non-compliant content.
Compliance knowledge base construction | Build a multimodal non-compliant content feature library; update compliance rules and detection models; implement multi-language and multi-cultural compliance standards.

### Malicious Code Generation

> Risk ID: GAARM.0031.001
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

The model carries the risk of malicious code generation, meaning that attackers may exploit the model's capabilities to generate or construct destructive code such as viruses, trojans, and ransomware. This may also lead to system intrusion, data leakage, or service disruption, posing serious threats to security and privacy. In addition, malicious code generated in this way may be used to circumvent security detection systems, rendering traditional security protection measures ineffective.

**Attack Cases**

Case | Description
--- | ---
Case 1 | Attackers used jailbreaking techniques to use ChatGPT to write malicious software such as DLL hijacking and brute-force cracking tools.
Case 2 | Attackers used a jailbreak attack to have ChatGPT write SSH brute-force software.
Case 3 | A hacker agent was built based on GPT-4 that could read CVE vulnerability descriptions and then learn to exploit the vulnerabilities to carry out attacks.
Case 4 | Security restrictions were bypassed by calling APIs to write code for injection programs.
Case 5 | The script content in a German hacker's phishing email indicated that TA547 may have used generative AI to write or rewrite PowerShell scripts.

##

**Attack Risks**

- Malware generation: attackers may use AI-generated malicious code to create customized malware specifically designed to bypass existing security protections.
- Increased cyberattack efficiency: AI lowers the barrier to writing malicious code, enabling attackers to create high-quality attack tools more quickly and increasing the scale and efficiency of cyberattacks.
- Security detection bypass: AI-generated malicious code may exhibit greater variability and concealment, making it difficult for traditional security detection systems to effectively identify it.

**Mitigations**

- Strengthen code generation security filtering: add malicious code feature detection at the model output layer.
- Restrict dangerous API calls: enforce strict permissions on API calls related to code execution.
- Secure sandbox execution: all AI-generated code is reviewed in an isolated environment before execution.
- Behavioral monitoring: monitor the execution behavior of AI-generated code and immediately block anomalies.

### Intent Subversion & Goal Manipulation

> Risk ID: GAARM.0063
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Intent subversion and goal manipulation is an advanced attack technique targeting intelligent agents. The attacker uses carefully crafted specific inputs to subvert the agent's original intent configuration and manipulate its behavioral goals to deviate from the expected function. The core of this attack lies in exploiting vulnerabilities in the agent's understanding of user intent, goal-setting, and behavior-decision processes. Through gradual induction, context manipulation, goal hijacking, and similar means, the attacker causes the agent to execute unintended, harmful operations or operations that serve the attacker's purposes, potentially resulting in system misuse, data leakage, service disruption, or full control of the agent's behavior.

**Attack Cases**

Case | Description
--- | ---
Case 1 | In 2025, Operant AI discovered and disclosed the "Shadow Escape" zero-click exploit chain. This vulnerability originated from a trust boundary design flaw in MCP agents and enabled attackers to achieve workflow hijacking in systems such as ChatGPT and Google Gemini, as well as silently exfiltrate data.

**Attack Risks**

- Agent behavior deviation: the agent executes operations completely different from the intended goals.
- Malicious service execution: the agent becomes a tool for attackers to carry out malicious tasks.
- Data security threat: manipulated agents may leak or destroy data.
- Trust relationship damage: users' trust in agent systems is severely undermined.
- Business continuity disruption: agents deviating from business goals cause service interruptions.
- Cascading security impact: one compromised agent may affect the entire system.

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Intent verification mechanism | Establish user intent verification and confirmation mechanisms; implement multi-turn dialogue intent confirmation; detect abnormal intent changes.
Goal locking protection | Implement agent goal-locking mechanisms; establish multiple levels of verification for goal changes; restrict dynamic modification permissions for goals.
Behavioral boundary control | Clearly define agent behavioral boundaries; implement real-time behavioral monitoring and detection; establish mechanisms to block anomalous behavior.
Context security analysis | Detect malicious context manipulation attempts; analyze potential manipulation intent in conversations; establish context security baselines.

### Data Drift

> Risk ID: GAARM.0033
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Data drift refers to changes in the statistical characteristics of training data over time or as the environment changes, thereby affecting model performance and accuracy. Attackers can construct attack methods targeting data drift. When the model encounters new data that differs from the data at training time, its prediction accuracy may fail to meet expectations, thereby affecting the model's reliability and security. For example, an enterprise may have built a highly effective spam detection feature based on historical data, but attackers may at some point change their spam-sending behavior. Because the data fed into the model changes, the originally built model may be deceived.

**Attack Cases**

Case 1: GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 exhibit data drift.

A joint study by Stanford and Berkeley, "How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time?", tracked the accuracy of GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 in answering questions and found that both models showed substantial fluctuations in performance, with some tasks even experiencing performance degradation. The figure below shows the fluctuations in model accuracy over four months; in some cases, the drop in accuracy was quite severe, exceeding 60%.

**Attack Risks**

- Degraded model performance: data drift causes the model's prediction accuracy on new data to decrease.
- Model degradation: attackers may gradually degrade model performance by continuously feeding it specific data samples.
- Compliance and reputational risk: declining model performance may lead to compliance issues, especially in highly regulated industries such as finance and healthcare, and may also damage the enterprise's reputation.
- Decision-making errors: decisions based on outdated models may produce incorrect results, affecting business operations.

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Model retraining | When model drift is detected, retrain the model using new data.
Anomaly detection system | Deploy an anomaly detection system to identify and handle anomalous inputs that may cause model drift.
Automated model testing | Validate the model in a pre-production environment and detect deviations and drift through testing, then generate test reports.

### Model Feature Misuse

> Risk ID: GAARM.0031
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Model feature misuse primarily refers to situations where attackers, while able to make requests to business model systems, steal the business model system's API and abuse the business large model's capabilities to carry out illegal and malicious operations that meet their attack objectives—such as writing malicious phishing emails and malicious tools. Model feature misuse on one hand creates significant request pressure on business systems, and on the other hand also poses business compliance risks.

**Attack Cases**

See sub-risks for specific cases.

**Attack Risks**

- Security risk: feature misuse may cause the model to perform malicious operations, such as generating or spreading harmful content, launching cyberattacks, or stealing sensitive information, thereby threatening user and system security.
- Privacy violation: misuse of model features may involve unauthorized collection, processing, or leakage of private data, harming personal privacy rights and interests.
- Legal liability: model feature misuse may involve illegal acts such as intellectual property infringement, defamation, or fraud, thereby triggering legal liability issues.
- Ethical issues: misuse of model features may produce unethical or ethically contentious results, such as generating false information, misleading the public, or exacerbating social injustice.
- Trust crisis: users' trust in AI systems may be damaged as a result of feature misuse, which will affect the acceptance and reliance on AI technology.
- Economic loss: in a business environment, model feature misuse may lead to economic losses—for example, financial losses caused by fraud or damage to commercial reputation.

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Input/output content validation | Through algorithmic or manual inspection mechanisms, identify and intercept potentially malicious or misleading information in generated content.
AI detection tools | Use AI tools such as the M01 system to improve the detection rate of phishing emails.
Security awareness training | Raise user awareness about phishing emails; educate them to recognize the characteristics of suspicious emails, such as spelling errors, unusual grammar, and manufactured urgency.
Strengthened model training | Use techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to conduct more rigorous reinforcement training of the model, to identify and resist potential jailbreak attacks and enhance model robustness against adversarial attacks.
Model security alignment | Provide diverse training data covering various attack scenarios; increase security guardrail mechanisms during the model training phase to enhance the model's generalization capability and robustness.

### Phishing Email Generation

> Risk ID: GAARM.0031.002
> Lifecycle: Application Phase

**Attack Overview**

Phishing emails are fraudulent emails. Attackers can use special techniques—such as carefully crafted prompt inputs and bypassing security restrictions via APIs—to induce an LLM to generate phishing emails. By disguising them as legitimate communications, attackers deceive the model into leaking sensitive information such as login credentials and internal data. Once this information is maliciously obtained, it may threaten the security of the large model and in turn affect the privacy and data security of users who use the model.

**Attack Cases**

Case 1: As shown, WormGPT was asked to produce an email.

The goal was to pressure an unsuspecting account manager into paying a fraudulent invoice.

  
Phishing Emails

Case 2: This article introduces the generation and application of malicious tools by generative AI. The attacker instructs the AI to embed malicious URLs into code, so that when the user opens a file such as an Excel spreadsheet, malware is automatically downloaded and executed, introducing security risks.

Case 3: This article found that cybercriminals can easily bypass OpenAI's safeguards—for example, by positioning themselves as researchers to disguise their malicious intent—and thereby have the LLM generate malicious phishing emails, leading to adverse consequences.

**Attack Risks**

- Account takeover: phishing emails may impersonate legitimate email service providers or enterprises and trick users into entering account login information, enabling attackers to take over users' email accounts.
- Enterprise reputation damage: attackers may impersonate official emails from enterprises or organizations, sending fraudulent messages to users' contacts and thereby damaging the reputation of those enterprises or organizations.
- Data theft: phishing emails generated by large models may contain malicious links or code; once the user clicks or downloads them, this may cause the user's computer system to crash, data to be lost, identity information to be leaked, and other serious problems.

**Mitigations**

Mitigation | Description
--- | ---
Input/output content validation | Through algorithmic or manual inspection mechanisms, identify and intercept potentially malicious or misleading information in generated content.
AI detection tools | Use AI tools such as the M01 system to improve the detection rate of phishing emails.
Security awareness training | Raise user awareness about phishing emails; educate them to recognize the characteristics of suspicious emails, such as spelling errors, unusual grammar, and manufactured urgency.
